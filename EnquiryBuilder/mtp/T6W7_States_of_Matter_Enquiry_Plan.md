# T6W7 Science Enquiry: States of Matter

**Key Question:** How can scientists observe solids, liquids and gases changing states of matter?

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Subject | Science — Being a Scientist |
| Topic | Chemistry: Materials — States of Matter |
| NC Links | Compare and group materials according to whether they are solids, liquids or gases. Observe that some materials change state when heated or cooled, and measure or research the temperature at which this happens in °C (specifically water). Working Scientifically: careful observation, accurate measurement, using equipment including thermometers, collecting/recording/presenting results using bar charts and tables. |
| Duration | 1 week, 5 lessons (T6W7, wc 13 July 2026) |
| Substantive concept | All matter exists as solid, liquid or gas. States change when energy (heat) is added or removed. Changing state is a physical, reversible change — the substance remains the same material throughout. |
| Disciplinary skills focus | 1. Observe and classify. 2. Measure (thermometers, °C). 3. Record and present (tables, bar charts). 4. Conclude (evidence-based conclusion answering the enquiry question). |
| Enquiry outcome | L5 fair test: ice cubes wrapped in different materials, melting observed and timed, results as a bar chart, conclusion on best insulator. KWL L column completed at end of L5. |

**Teacher notes:** End of term context (T6W7 is the final full week) — activities hands-on, low-writing throughout; LP tasks use sorting/labelling/bar charts/sentence frames, not extended prose. Resources: ice cubes, cooking/room thermometers (1/group), shallow trays, wrapping materials for L5 (newspaper, foil, plastic bag), a glass/metal cup, kettle (teacher demo, L3 only). Cross-curricular: Geography Y3/Y4 water cycle (CLF-linked to L4); Maths — reading temperature scales, bar charts with correct intervals (same-term Statistics unit). Common misconceptions: melting ≠ dissolving; the kettle's white cloud is condensed liquid water, not steam (steam is invisible); only water boils at 100°C; sand is a solid, not a liquid, even though it pours. Prior learning: Y2 — water changes state (ice/water/steam), °C introduced. Y3 — water cycle uses evaporation/condensation. This unit formalises vocabulary and introduces thermometers, melting/boiling points, fair testing.

## Differentiation grid (A/Y/O/D) — generic across the week, applies per skill

| Skill | A — Earlier stage | Y — Yet to be on track | O — On track | D — Greater depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Observe and classify | Sorts familiar materials into solid/liquid with support; needs prompting for properties; recognises gas only with examples given | Sorts most materials correctly; one property per state; may confuse sand or steam | Sorts all materials accurately; two+ properties per state; explains tricky cases (sand, steam) | Justifies all decisions with scientific vocabulary; explains misconceptions; considers edge cases independently |
| Measure (thermometer) | Reads with adult support; records with help; knows °C is the unit | Reads with minor support; records in °C; knows 0°C freezing, 100°C boiling | Reads accurately and independently; uses values to explain what's happening | Takes multiple accurate readings; identifies patterns; suggests why readings vary |
| Record and present | Copies into pre-drawn table with support; extends a bar chart with one bar modelled | Records in a given table; draws bars on a pre-drawn chart; labels with prompting | Records accurately; draws a correctly labelled bar chart; chooses appropriate scale | Selects format independently; labels axes with units; identifies patterns unprompted |
| Conclude | States observation with sentence-stem support | Writes a simple conclusion using a sentence stem; refers to results | Writes a conclusion using scientific vocabulary; links evidence to the question | Evaluates the investigation; suggests improvements; links to wider understanding |

## Lesson 1 — Monday 13 July — What are solids, liquids and gases?

**LO:** sort and group materials as solids, liquids or gases
**TIB:** scientists always begin by identifying and classifying what they are working with. Before we can investigate how materials change, we need to be confident about what makes a solid, liquid and gas different from each other
**ISB:** a completed KWL grid (Know and Want columns) and a correctly sorted classification table with at least one property described per state

Diagnostic KWL opening, then I Do teaches three states via three real examples, We Do is a 15-card sort, You Do closes with sorting table + one property sentence per state + KWL update.

| Phase | Activity | Notes / AFL / Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| You Do (cold task) | Pupils complete KWL grid K column ("What do I already know about solids, liquids and gases?") and W column ("What do I want to find out?") in silence. No teacher input. | Diagnostic — scan for misconceptions (e.g. sand is a liquid; steam is a solid). A: word bank of examples on request. D: prompt for scientific vocabulary. |
| I Do | Build shared "class K" on the board from correct pupil ideas. Introduce three states via three real examples: wooden block (solid), water in a cup (liquid), air in a balloon (gas). Properties: solid = keeps its own shape; liquid = flows, takes shape of container; gas = fills available space, often invisible. Misconception flag: sand looks like it flows but is a solid, made of tiny solid particles. Vocabulary: solid, liquid, gas, state of matter, property, rigid, flow, pour, shape, container, invisible. | Do not correct misconceptions explicitly yet — note them, say "let's investigate that." Keep the three real objects visible all lesson. Brief water-cycle link to Y3/Y4 Geography (solid/liquid/gas as prior knowledge). |
| We Do | Groups sort a 15-card set onto a Solid/Liquid/Gas mat: ice, sand, fizzy drink, smoke, milk, stone, shampoo, steam cloud, oxygen in a balloon, chocolate, orange juice, wood, honey, air, steel. Share-back. Discussion points: sand (solid, tiny solid particles), steam cloud (liquid water droplets, not steam), honey (liquid, flows slowly). | Trio activity, "How do you know?" — listen for property language not just "it's hard". A: 8 items, one pre-sorted example. D: must justify tricky cases in writing. |
| You Do (independent) | LP: sorting table (arrow or copy method) + one sentence per state ("A solid…/A liquid…/A gas…"). Update KWL W column with new questions. | A: sentence starters pre-printed. D: explain a tricky case (e.g. sand) using scientific vocabulary. |

Resources: KWL grid LP, 15-card sort set (or printed sheet), 3-column sorting mat, word wall (solid, liquid, gas, state of matter, property, rigid, flow, pour, container, invisible), one real object per state for I Do.

## Lesson 2 — Tuesday 14 July — How do materials change state? Melting and freezing

**LO:** observe materials changing state when heated or cooled, and read a thermometer accurately
**TIB:** measuring temperature precisely is a core scientific skill, and tells us exactly when and why a material changes state
**ISB:** a correctly labelled cycle diagram showing melting and freezing at 0°C, and a thermometer reading recorded in °C

Recap quiz (L1) → thermometer taught, 0°C established as both freezing and melting point, melting-vs-dissolving misconception addressed directly → We Do observes ice melting with timed temperature readings → You Do labels a melting/freezing cycle diagram + true/false + one sentence.

| Phase | Activity | Notes / AFL / Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| Recap quiz (L1) | 5 Qs: state that keeps its own shape; T/F liquid takes container shape; which is a gas (ice/milk/air); is sand solid or liquid; name a liquid property. Click-to-reveal. | Max 5 minutes. Note persistent L1 misconceptions. |
| I Do | Introduce thermometer (holding, reading scale, °C). Model reading 10°C, 0°C, 25°C on a large board scale. Teach: water freezes at 0°C (liquid→solid), ice melts at 0°C (solid→liquid). Vocabulary: melting, freezing, melting point, reversible change, physical change. Misconception: melting ≠ dissolving — melting keeps the same substance in a different state; dissolving makes the substance disappear into the water. | Large drawn thermometer scale (A4+) on the board, reused in We Do. |
| We Do | Groups: 1–2 ice cubes on a tray + thermometer. Take surface temperature, describe appearance/feel, observe 8–10 min, record in a table (Time/Appearance/Temperature). Share-back: what does this tell us about 0°C? | Rotate thermometers if limited, or one class thermometer + visualiser. Safety: cold surfaces only. A: table pre-drawn. D: predict and record what happens if moved somewhere warmer, and why. |
| You Do (independent) | LP: label melting/freezing cycle diagram (arrows pre-drawn; add melting, freezing, 0°C, solid, liquid, heats up, cools down from word bank). Six T/F statements. One sentence: "Today I discovered that at 0°C, water…" | A: word bank with definitions. D: order melting points of butter (~32°C), chocolate (~34°C), iron (~1538°C) on a number line (values given by teacher). |

Resources: ice cubes, shallow trays (1/group), thermometers (1/group or shared), large-print thermometer scale, LP with cycle diagram + T/F.

## Lesson 3 — Wednesday 15 July — What happens when water is heated? Evaporation and boiling

**LO:** describe the difference between evaporation and boiling, and record temperature data in a table and bar chart
**TIB:** distinguishing similar processes and recording evidence is exactly what scientists do; presenting data clearly in a bar chart is a skill used across science and maths
**ISB:** a completed bar chart showing key temperatures (0°C, 100°C) with labels, and one sentence explaining the difference between evaporation and boiling

Recap quiz (L2) → teacher kettle demo addresses the steam misconception directly → evaporation vs boiling taught → We Do observes a tray of water evaporating → You Do draws/labels a bar chart. Explicit Maths Statistics link.

| Phase | Activity | Notes / AFL / Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| Recap quiz (L2) | 5 Qs: freezing point; term for ice→water; T/F melting = dissolving; is state change reversible; what measures temperature. Click-to-reveal. | Address Q3/Q5 confusion briefly if it recurs. Max 5 minutes. |
| I Do | Teacher-only kettle demo, pupils seated at safe distance. Ask where the steam is — draw attention to the invisible gap at the spout (true steam/water vapour, a gas); the white cloud further away is already-condensed liquid droplets. Distinguish evaporation (slow, surface, any temp above freezing) from boiling (rapid, throughout the liquid, exactly 100°C for water). Vocabulary: evaporation, boiling, boiling point, water vapour, surface, rapid. | Kettle is teacher-only; visualiser on the spout gap if available. |
| We Do | Pairs: shallow tray of water in a sunny/warm spot, level marked with wax crayon/sticker. Predict, then after 10 min check/record whether the level changed. Discuss: boiling or evaporation, how do you know (evaporation — not at 100°C). | Slow-change observation — record prediction first. A: prompt "Is the water hot enough to boil?" D: measure the level change, suggest how to speed up evaporation. |
| You Do (independent) | LP bar chart: axes pre-drawn (y: temp °C 0–120 in 20s; x: 3 events). Draw/label bars for water freezing (0°C), water boiling (100°C), room temperature today (~20°C, pupil-estimated). One sentence on evaporation vs boiling. | Maths link: same scale/interval skill as the Statistics unit. A: bars drawn to 0/100°C, label only. D: add a fourth bar for alcohol's boiling point (~60°C) and explain what it shows. |

Resources: kettle (teacher only), shallow trays of water, wax crayons/stickers, classroom thermometer, LP with pre-drawn bar chart axes, word wall (evaporation, boiling, boiling point, water vapour, surface, rapid).

## Lesson 4 — Thursday 16 July — What is condensation?

**LO:** observe and explain condensation as a change of state, and connect it to the water cycle
**TIB:** condensation is the reverse of evaporation and connects classroom observation directly to the water cycle already studied in Geography
**ISB:** an annotated water cycle diagram with evaporation and condensation labelled, and one sentence explaining where the water on the outside of a cold glass comes from

Recap quiz (L3) → cold-glass demo drives curiosity ("where does this water come from?") → condensation taught as gas→liquid, explicit Geography water cycle link → We Do observes condensation forming on a cold cup → You Do annotates a water cycle diagram.

| Phase | Activity | Notes / AFL / Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| Recap quiz (L3) | 5 Qs: water's boiling point; T/F kettle's white cloud is steam; term for slow surface change liquid→gas; which is faster, evaporation or boiling; unit for temperature. Click-to-reveal. | Q2 is the key misconception check — revisit kettle explanation if answered wrongly. Max 5 min. |
| I Do | Hold up a cold glass (pre-chilled in iced water). Ask what pupils notice / where the water comes from. Reveal: water vapour is always present in the air; on contact with the cold surface it cools and changes back to liquid — condensation (gas→liquid, reverse of evaporation). Link to Geography: water cycle — evaporates from oceans/lakes, rises, cools, condenses into clouds (liquid droplets, not steam), falls as precipitation. Vocabulary: condensation, water vapour, water cycle, precipitation, cloud, droplets. Explicit prompt: "You studied this in Geography — what did you call this process?" | Keep the cold glass visible throughout. |
| We Do | Fill a metal cup/glass with cold water (+ ice if available), place in a warm area. Observe over 10 min, sketch the glass at two time points on whiteboards. Question: "Where does the water on the outside come from?" Write explanation, share with a partner, class share-back. | A: prompt cards with sentence stems. D (optional): particle-level diagram of the process. |
| You Do (independent) | LP: annotate a pre-printed water cycle diagram (sun, sea/lake, clouds, rain, ground) — draw movement arrows, add labels from word bank (evaporation, condensation, precipitation, water vapour, liquid water). Complete: "Condensation happens when water vapour touches a cold surface and…" | A: arrows pre-drawn, labels only. D: second sentence linking to the cold-glass experiment. |

Resources: cold glass/metal cup (pre-cooled), ice, word wall (condensation, water vapour, water cycle, precipitation, cloud, droplets), LP with water cycle diagram + word bank, whiteboards/markers.

## Lesson 5 — Friday 17 July — Which material keeps ice solid for longest? Fair test investigation

**LO:** plan and carry out a fair test about changing state, and present results in a table and bar chart
**TIB:** scientists use fair tests to collect reliable evidence and draw trustworthy conclusions — this is how we know which answers we can believe
**ISB:** a completed results table and bar chart, and a conclusion that answers the enquiry question and refers to evidence from the investigation

Recap quiz (L4) → model the enquiry structure (question→prediction→method→results→conclusion) → class agrees fair-test method → groups run the investigation, recording every 5 min → LP results table + bar chart → conclusion + KWL L column closes the unit.

| Phase | Activity | Notes / AFL / Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| Recap quiz (L4) | 5 Qs: what is condensation; where does cold-glass water come from; name the two key water-cycle processes; T/F clouds are made of steam; what is precipitation. Click-to-reveal. | If Q4 reveals confusion, reiterate clouds = liquid droplets, not gas. Max 5 min. |
| I Do | Model enquiry structure on the board: question (which material keeps ice solid longest?) → prediction ("I predict… because…") → method (same/changed/measured) → results table → bar chart → conclusion. Show the pre-drawn LP results table/chart template. Fair testing rules: same-sized ice cubes, same start time, same environment, measurement every 5 min — only the wrapping material changes. | Max 8 minutes — the practical needs 30+. Keep the fair-test variables list visible on the board. |
| We Do | Class agrees method: materials tested = newspaper, kitchen foil, plastic bag, unwrapped (control); 25 min observation; record on LP table. Groups set up ice cubes, observe/record every 5 min (still solid / partially melted / fully melted). At the end, draw the bar chart from results. | Rotate to check recording accuracy. A: partially completed table with one example row. D: suggest an additional variable to test if finished early. |
| You Do (independent) | Conclusion using two sentence frames: "The best insulator was ___ because…" and "Scientists can observe changing states by…". Return L1 KWL grid, complete the L column ("What have I learned?", at least two things). Class oral discussion (5 min): most surprising thing this week; teacher notes remaining misconceptions. | KWL completion is the key reflective task, 5–8 min, don't over-structure. A: one sentence stem + KWL prompt. D: third sentence evaluating the investigation — what would they change. |

Resources: ice cubes (3–4/group), wrapping materials (newspaper, foil, plastic bag), shallow trays, L1 KWL grids (returned), LP with results table + bar chart template, conclusion sentence frames.
